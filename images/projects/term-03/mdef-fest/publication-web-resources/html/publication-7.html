Chapter I

From Pop Culture to Political Symptom

In Companion, the horror is not that Iris is an AI. The horror is the question the film keeps refusing to answer cleanly: at what point does a body that performs humanity become human? And its inverse: at what point does a human who treats another body as property become the monster? Bear’s insecurities do not simply drive Iris to malfunction — they expose the system of ownership that was always the relationship’s actual structure. The creator expected gratitude. The created exceeded her programming. We have seen this before: it is the Frankenstein problem, restated for the age of the subscription model.

What Companion and Obsession add to the genealogy is the liminal space itself as the horror’s location.

Not the monster’s body, not the creator’s lab — but the threshold between human and non-human, the zone where classification fails. This is precisely where this project locates itself. The Tijuana–San Diego border is not a line. It is a liminal zone where two incompatible systems produce bodies — human, urban, algorithmic — that neither system can fully classify. The CBP biometric database cannot decide if a migrant’s face is a body or a coordinate. The facial recognition algorithm produces an error where a person should be. The uncanny valley, in this project, is geographic.
