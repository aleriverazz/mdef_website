Chapter I

Monster Genealogy: From the Abnormal to the Fabricated Anomaly

Historically the figure of the monster has served as an incarnation of the abnormality. In the late 1800s, Monster Culture was only based on physical abnormality. By 100 years later, you can say that modern science in some way “killed” the physical monster, with medical research, medication, prevention and vaccination, shifting the meaning of the word monster.

MONSTROSITY is no longer a physical form, but rather an action — not even the actor by itself, but the action committed. Foucault frames the monster as an anomaly that exposes the fragility of normality, a juridical-natural entity in the biopower chain. In this project I focus on the idea that in the present, monsters are not even actions, but places — created by actions in an entanglement of events. But the anomaly is also, as Foucault explains, an open door for possibilities. As Jeffrey Jerome Cohen declares: we live in a Time of Monsters. But where and what are the monsters of this present?

But something has shifted. In the age of Byung-Chul Han’s Psychopolitics, the monster is no longer projected outward. The monster has been internalized, domesticated, made profitable. Sayak Valencia, in her theory of Capitalismo Gore, identifies how necropower operates not just on the margins but as a structuring logic of the global economy — where the production of death, violence, and disposability is not an accident of capitalism but its engine. The border, the favela, the maquiladora: these are not exceptions. They are the prototype.

The new monster is not the creature at the gate. It is the algorithm that decides your credit score. It is the filter that “improves” your face before you post it. It is the deepfake of your voice generated without your consent. It is the biometric database that stores a migrant’s identity while their body waits in a detention center.

The monster, in 2025, wears a clean interface.
