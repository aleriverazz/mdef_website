Chapter I

From Pop Culture to Political Symptom

Pop culture has always been the monster’s first address. Long before theory, cinema and music named the crisis. Think of the Twilight saga; not as escapism but as a symptom. The vampire, historically the monstrous aristocrat, the foreigner who drains resources and cannot die, became, in Stephenie Meyer’s hands, a domesticated ideal: beautiful, immortal, controlled, wealthy, white. The monster became aspirational. Or consider the zombie genre’s explosion post-2008: The Walking Dead or World War Z — arriving precisely at the moment global financial collapse made visible how precarious “normal” life actually was. The horde is not the threat. You are already in the horde, moving between three jobs in a solitary, already utilitarian society.

More recently, Poor Things (2023) gives us Bella Baxter — stitched together, reanimated, educated from scratch, thrust into a world that wants to own her body and narrate her identity. Unlike the Frankenstein monster, who is monstrous because he was made, Bella is monstrous because she refuses to perform normality. She eats, she fucks, she learns, she leaves, without any “correctness social manners”. Is the monster in Poor Things Bella? — No, it’s clear the monster is Godwin, the God-man who created her and expects gratitude. The monster is the system of creation and ownership, not the created. I don’t think it is a casual thought that the monster realtor is a man and creates a feminine persona.
