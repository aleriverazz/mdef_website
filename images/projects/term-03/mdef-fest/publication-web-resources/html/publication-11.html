Chapter II

Monster Cartographies: Reading Culture Through the Stack

If Chapter I established the genealogy of the monster, Chapter II proposes a method. Not a taxonomy. Not a classification. A cartography.

The distinction matters. A taxonomy fixes. A cartography moves. It traces flows, fractures, and the places where systems fail to contain what they produced. This project does not want to classify monsters. It wants to map where they live — and more precisely, where they reveal that the ground was never stable to begin with.

The tool for this mapping is Benjamin Bratton’s Stack: a theoretical model of planetary computation organized in six layers — Earth, Cloud, City, Address, Interface, User. Bratton conceived the Stack as a way to understand how software and sovereignty operate simultaneously across scales. For this project, the Stack becomes something else: a spatial grammar for reading where monsters operate, where crises crystallize, and where the classification systems that organize bodies, territories, and identities begin to fail.

Each layer produces its own monster. Each layer has its own geography of fracture.
